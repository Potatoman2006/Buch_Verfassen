# The Kingdom of Avarenne

Avarenne fills the Lume's broad grain valleys with orchards, horse pastures, river cities, ruined imperial avenues, and villages whose church towers rise out of morning mist. Foreigners call it the bread kingdom. Its inhabitants also know it as the country of white geese, summer dust, blue funeral cloth, and the long arguments that begin when a noble announces what everyone supposedly remembers. Its crown is powerful because many people need it, and precarious because those needs conflict.

## Fields and cities

The Golden Levee country grows wheat, barley, beans, and flax behind old river banks. Households share ovens and organise the hardest harvest work across several families. A good meal is bread torn into broth, sharp cheese, pickled greens, and enough time to argue after eating. Children race geese through village lanes; grandmothers teach elaborate insults disguised as harvesting songs. Most families speak Lumic, with local vowel shifts that locate a stranger more quickly than their coat.

Lume's upper city districts house court servants, scholars, artisans, and noble households whose country estates lie far away. Humans are numerous, but Aelri vine growers, Urven cavalry families, Ruun gardeners, and Kharad craft households have lived in Avarenne for generations. In the eastern horse country, mounted households treat seasonal movement between pasture and town as normal. They share more daily practice with some Redwind neighbours than with an Avarenne silk merchant.

Royal cities favour public processions and fashionable sincerity. Rural communities honour the quiet skill of someone who can deliver a difficult foal or judge rain from the smell of soil. The kingdom is not divided neatly into decadent nobles and virtuous peasants. A landlord may protect a village against an officer; a village may persecute a neighbour with nowhere else to go.

## A queen among cousins

Queen Celene Serault rules through a network of noble houses, cities, temple lands, and personal alliances. Her claim is accepted, but a younger cousin, Prince Odran, is a successful frontier commander with an attractive promise: restore the kingdom's ancient reach and stop bargaining with smaller neighbours. Celene fears another war like the War of the Two Harvests. Odran believes her caution invites border raids and humiliation. People who lost relatives to raiders can support him without desiring an empire.

The immediate dispute is a Crownspine approach whose villages speak Lumic, Rhyd, and Vornic in overlapping combinations. An expedition presented as protection could become occupation. Celene needs Rhydder's passage and Kharad cooperation; her cousin hopes to force her into endorsing a victory already begun. Meanwhile several lowland nobles hold back horses because summer illness has weakened their herds. The court's bright ceremonies conceal a kingdom whose military choices are constrained by people and animals that cannot simply be summoned.

## Faith under an open roof

The Covenant of Lamps blesses seed in village courtyards and maintains city shelters. The queen receives a lamp at her recognition, then carries it herself for the length of the public hall. Priests debate whether the gesture obliges the crown to shelter every fugitive. Root Communion rites survive strongly among vine growers and river woods; noble families often sponsor both practices.

At the Barrow of the Uncrowned Daughter, women bring blue cloth for relatives denied a proper funeral. Tradition calls the buried woman a princess who refused to marry a conqueror. Excavated objects support an old high-status burial but do not prove the tale. The site matters because mourners have made it a place of recognition, not because a hidden royal ghost certifies every later political claim.

## Fertile magic, costly splendour

Growth practitioners use prepared soil, living cuttings, water, and close seasonal knowledge to encourage rooting or improve a sick plant's chance of survival. They cannot conjure a harvest from stone or replace exhausted soil. Noble gardens can flower out of season only in maintained sheltered beds with a cost in fuel, labour, and plant health. A village may resent the winter roses at court because its best grower has been hired away during a blight.

The old Palace of Rain contains Ilyran water stairs where falling droplets seem to hang in patterned veils under lamplight. Their apparent suspension comes from shaped flow and perception working through installed glass and stone. They are not stopped time. Portions still function under careful maintenance; others have become ivy-filled courts open to the sky. Celene uses one restored hall for audiences, giving visitors a glimpse of imperial beauty whose deeper expense servants understand.

The royal forests shelter great tusked boars and deer with unusually broad branching antlers. Hunting is politics as well as food. An invited guest may be honoured, watched, or deliberately placed where a frightened animal can reach them. A wounded boar requires no spell to turn a court ritual into a succession crisis.

## Places and neighbours

The city of Lume rises on terraces above a bend of the river, its lower streets full of ferrymen, food sellers, and washing lines invisible in formal portraits. The Golden Levee holds orchard chapels and earthworks older than remembered dynasties. The Horse Stars are pale stones set across eastern pasture, useful as gathering places even though their original purpose is uncertain. The Barrow of the Uncrowned Daughter stands near a pilgrim road bordered by blue flowers planted by generations of mourners.

Orison's river passage feeds the kingdom's cities and gives outsiders a means to resist royal demands. Rhydder's roads turn mountain policy into lived danger. Kharad tools help harvest the grain with which Avarenne hopes to influence Kharad decisions. Merenne needs that grain after storms but remembers the cost of the last embargo. The queen's choices therefore enter distant kitchens long before her banners appear.

## Open stories

A horse healer joins a prince's expedition and discovers how illness is being hidden from its soldiers. Two sisters inherit an orchard divided by rival noble loyalties. A Lamp priest must shelter a deserter whose victim also comes seeking help. A court gardener is asked to make a dead tree bloom for a ceremony and refuses an impossible promise. A young noble attends a hunt intended to secure a marriage and returns with responsibility for a death no faction can afford to describe honestly.
