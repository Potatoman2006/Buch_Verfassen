# The Rhydder Marches

Rhydder is a country of high saddles, rain-dark slate, sheep tracks, and roads whose milestones vanish into winter snow. Six marcher valleys meet at Gildenward, where bells from different temples can be heard across the same windy market. Its people speak of the road as a guest who must be fed: useful, demanding, and capable of bringing enemies to the door. That image shapes hospitality more deeply than any single ruling house.

## Valley hearths and high camps

Lower valleys grow oats, rye, peas, and cabbage around thick-walled farmhouses. Families sleep close to animals in the hardest weather and move their beds away from the damp spring wall. Cheese, wool, lime, and slate leave Rhydder by pack train; grain and tools return. A household's pride may be a dry roof, a daughter who knows two passes, or a fiddle carried safely through three generations. Weddings often begin as serious family gatherings and end in rowdy competitions to invent the least flattering verse about the couple.

Torvane's road towns are crowded, practical, and outward looking. Human and Ruun households run inns and workshops beside Kharad stoneworkers and Urven mule breeders. Rhyd dominates at home, while Low Meridian serves the road. Farther north, Blue Cairn's upland camps move between summer pasture and sheltered winter hamlets. Thalen herders and human kin share grazing knowledge there. Their Rhyd contains weather terms that lowlanders treat as poetry and herders treat as instructions that may save a life.

Road towns consider a stranger useful until proved otherwise. High camps ask who guided that stranger over the last ridge. Neither attitude is universal kindness or cruelty. A hospitable inn can exploit a trapped traveller; a suspicious shepherd can spend a night finding a lost child. Children from both communities grow up knowing that a winter journey requires someone else to expect their arrival.

## Power at the passes

House Torvane supplies the leading marcher captain, confirmed by the six valleys and traditionally acknowledged by Avarenne. Captain Elian Torvane wants a permanent force large enough to protect roads against winter raiders and armed deserters. The valley elders fear that such a force will become a private army. Elian's supporters remember burned hostels; his opponents remember the previous captain using an escort to settle a family feud.

Avarenne's court wants Rhydder's southern passes ready for a royal expedition toward a disputed Crownspine approach. Several marcher families have relatives on the far side. Closing the road may prevent a war but strand grain that the upper valleys need. Opening it may feed those families while enabling their conquest. The dispute divides households, and neither the queen's officers nor Rhydder's elders can predict how long a marching army will remain.

## Faith carried in a pocket

Roadside Witnesses leave a smooth stone at the last safe shelter before departure and retrieve it on return. A stone left through winter is a reason to ask after someone, not proof that they have died. The Covenant of Lamps runs valley hospices, but many high camps pray outdoors beneath a turned bowl held to keep the wind off a flame. Blue Cairn burials use cairns because deep digging is difficult; mourners sing the dead person's least dignified remembered habit alongside their virtues.

Some priests teach that the missing walk behind an unseen road beyond the snow. Authors may use that as belief. The stable fact is that remembered-form disturbances sometimes repeat footsteps near old paving, especially after thaw. They do not lead unerringly to missing people. Hospices warn travellers to test a path with a pole instead of trusting either a familiar voice or a comforting story.

## Stone craft and its price

Rhydder masons learn a narrow stone practice from Kharad teachers. Chalk slurry, iron pins, measured tapping, and sustained attention can reveal and temporarily reduce strain around an existing fracture. They cannot summon a bridge across a gorge. When a stoneworker stabilizes an arch, ordinary crews still unload it and replace damaged material. Fatigue makes late repairs dangerous, especially when military officers demand one more wagon before closure.

At the Hush Stair, Ilyran steps cut into a ravine hold sound strangely: a dropped pebble may seem to strike from behind. The effect stays within the old worked stone and weakens where weather has broken its pattern. Smugglers exploit confusion there; experienced guides tether novices through the worst section. The Stair's danger is not a monster waiting for a protagonist, but a place whose known risk people repeatedly accept because the alternative is another four days on the road.

## Places beyond the road signs

Gildenward's market shelters beneath an unfinished stone roof raised by rival valleys that still disagree about its last span. Blue Cairn's hospice keeps a warm room without asking a traveller's faith, though its fuel gathering falls heavily on nearby families. The Lamb's Teeth are narrow ridges used for summer grazing, dotted with red-painted shelters visible through rain. Beneath them, the Widow Lake preserves drowned tree trunks from an old landslide; no boatman likes the sound of his hull touching wood he cannot see.

Rhydder exchanges stone skill with Kharad, food with Avarenne, wool with Greyhaven, and seasonal labour with Orison. Border marriages carry practical knowledge in both directions. War would interrupt more than shipments: it would turn a grandmother's accent or a cousin's arrival into a cause for suspicion.

## Open stories

A young guide must choose between finishing a lucrative winter crossing and turning back for the weakest traveller. A hospice household discovers that its revered founder drove another family from the only warm valley field. Two siblings inherit a flock and disagree whether to settle in a road town. A stoneworker realizes that an army's intended crossing will fail, then must persuade a commander who suspects sabotage. A missing shepherd returns with an unfamiliar child and an explanation nobody quite believes; the child need not be supernatural for the welcome to matter.
