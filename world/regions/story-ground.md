# The Rookharbour corridor

This is a deep local module for the northeastern shore of Merenne, fixed on the first day of 250 IS. Rookharbour, Briarbridge, and Saint Orra's occupy one small district in a much larger country. Their households have histories before Mara Venn and futures independent of her. The autumn 250–255 story begins from this baseline; subsequent events belong to its dated continuity.

## The three places and the routes between them

Rookharbour stands on the coast, with Briarbridge 22 km northwest inland and Saint Orra's 16 km northeast along a wooded headland. Briarbridge and Saint Orra's are joined by a 30 km track running broadly east through broken forest. These are travelled distances, not straight lines. Rookharbour to Briarbridge takes an ordinary walker one long day, a loaded cart a day and a half, and a rider five to six hours without exhausting the horse. Rookharbour to Saint Orra's takes five to six hours on foot in good conditions. The forest connection needs a full summer day or two short winter days. The whole corridor fits within 40 km across; none of its roads approaches the other side of Merenne.

The Briar Brook runs southeast from the forest, passes under Briarbridge's two stone arches, and enters Rookharbour behind the western quay. Saint Orra's occupies a separate limestone spur east of the brook's drainage. Its old works connect to the sea through subterranean channels, never to the brook by an impossible uphill river. A rough shore path between town and headland floods around the middle of a rising tide. Boats can make the passage in two or three hours in favourable wind; a gale can prevent departure for days. The outer sea is western Inner Salt water, reached without crossing the ocean straits.

There are only three dependable daytime shelters along the monastery road: the charcoal burners' hut at the fourth kilometre, the abandoned lime shed at the ninth, and the monastery itself. None belongs to vampires. A mapped shelter may already hold a family, have lost its roof, or be locked. Covering sixteen kilometres before dawn requires a departure time and weather margin, not a narrative cut.

## Rookharbour's ordinary days

About 3,200 people live in the town and immediate quay lanes. Human families form the largest group; Ruun net makers, Kharad masons, Aelri sailors, and several Urven households are ordinary neighbours. Merennic is the home language, with Low Meridian heard on the quays. The town smells of tarred cord, apple skins, wet wool, and fish smoking beneath tiled hoods. Its buildings rise in uneven steps behind a low sea wall. Wealth is uphill; the poorest beds sit closest to water that sometimes comes through the floor.

Most households combine jobs. A fish seller dries seaweed for gardeners; a cooper's children help pick pears; a boat carpenter keeps goats on her sister's inland ground. Winter evenings move work indoors around shared lamps. Rookharbour's pleasure is communal and ungrand: warm cider after hauling, competitive bad songs, painted boat eyes, and children following the first returning swallow through the streets. Marriage often enlarges a working household rather than founding a separate home. Fosterage is common after sea deaths, but a fostered child can inherit affection without inheriting tools.

The Venn sisters occupy two small rooms over a rope shed near the western quay. Their mother, who repaired nets and sails, died of a winter fever in 245 IS. Their father was lost when a coastal cargo boat foundered during the Three Storms of 244. Neither loss is secretly supernatural. Mara, twenty in autumn 250, patches sailcloth, tends the sail loft's small charcoal stove, and sometimes accompanies coastal deliveries to make repairs aboard. She can sew, splice, read tide marks, and handle a modest boat; she has no formal magical training. Tessa, seventeen, works part of each week at the ropewalk and helps a neighbour tend kitchen beds. She is better with plants and blunt conversation than with a needle. Their affection includes arguments about Mara treating every danger as her private responsibility.

The sisters eat with the rope shed's owner, Widow Harl, twice weekly in exchange for mending. Harl has aching hands, a loud laugh, and no secret connection to the monastery. She exemplifies the assistance that makes precarious households possible. Minor residents may be developed in stories, but these four facts anchor the sisters: skilled ordinary work, real bereavement, thin financial margin, and neighbours who know their faces.

## Briarbridge and the forest edge

Briarbridge has approximately 450 permanent residents, rising during the fruit and charcoal seasons. Its east bank has orchards, a mill, and the village common; its west bank climbs into mixed beech and ancient dark-leaf oak. Humans and Aelri share several households, while Thalen seasonal gatherers maintain a sheltered camp upstream. Bough speech and Merennic overlap. A child's first language does not predict which side of the river their family considers home.

Root Communion rites concern pruning, burial beneath coppice, and leaving a spring's first rush undisturbed. A small Lamp house lights a window for late travellers. Most villagers see no contradiction between the two. The principal argument is whether the regent's demand for storm-repair timber justifies cutting farther into old woodland. Charcoal families need winter employment; foresters fear losing the root webs that hold the slope. Neither side can feed a child with an abstract principle.

Briarbridge's midwife and herb grower is locally trusted, though this module leaves her personal name open. Her practice includes fever care, clean water, splints, and identifying plants. It does not cure vampirism. The village can conceal a wounded traveller for a few nights, but prolonged concealment becomes a burden shared by people who have not necessarily consented. Its mill loft is dry, opaque, and occupied. The forest has caves, but most are shallow animal dens, seasonally wet, or dangerous to enter without scouting.

## Brother Cael and the town's response to vampires

Brother Cael is a human lay brother of the Covenant of Lamps, thirty-eight at the baseline. He works from Rookharbour's small shelter and infirmary. He has learned wound care and a narrow warding practice using scored fired clay, salt water, a lamp flame, and an accurately repeated pattern. His ward can expose disturbance around an existing remembered-form wound or reduce a local echo's sensory interference while he maintains it. It cannot repel a vampire merely for being a vampire. He also knows restraint, tracking, and the anatomical means of killing one. His tools make him a hunter; his choice to hear a frightened person makes him a healer.

Cael has encountered predatory vampires and knows that kindness cannot make blood unnecessary. He opposes killing an unviolent turned person simply for the condition, but will defend an unwilling donor with force. He cannot guarantee that the town agrees. Its wall captain fears panic and missing sailors; several bereaved families favour immediate execution; rescue crews are more accustomed to helping whoever can still be brought ashore. There is no standing donor society at the baseline and no hidden supply of safe blood. Creating one would require repeated informed consent, many healthy adults, long recovery, and practical care. Tessa alone cannot keep Mara alive safely.

An ordinary bite does not turn anyone. A dying adult must receive vampire blood and survive the transformation. A vampire's need for about 150–200 ml every three nights cannot be met by goodwill alone, and blood kept longer than six hours loses sustaining virtue. Rookharbour has no device that changes that fact. Darkness is shelter rather than invulnerability; cloudy daylight burns, and an opaque room can catch fire.

## Saint Orra's and the Listening Sill

Saint Orra's was a small coastal refuge monastery founded by followers of the historical healer after her death. It stood above much older Ilyran works. Its abandonment followed storm damage in 244, when the roof of the infirmary fell and the cliff stair became unsafe. No curse made its residents disappear. Surviving brothers and sisters moved to working houses; the unroofed chapel still attracts people who leave flowers for the sea dead.

Beneath the monastery lies the Listening Sill: three linked limestone chambers and approximately 600 metres of cut tidal channels. Its Ilyran builders diverted water into side chambers to reduce pressure at a landing and preserve a dry rock passage during selected tides. It was a local harbour work, never a regulator of the Inner Salt. Copper strips set in ceramic grooves carry a learned tide pattern between stone anchor beds. Fresh brine wets the grooves; intact stone and tidal motion supply the medium and available force. Echo chambers helped trained workers hear dangerous changes in resonance. Saltfall damaged the undercurrent here, leaving fixed sensory fragments of rushing feet, a bell, and water striking doors. They are impressions, not trapped souls. They cannot answer questions or reveal information they never held.

At baseline, two outer anchor beds are cracked and the inland relief channel is blocked by storm debris. The main stone shutter can be moved by three people at separate hand wheels when pressure is low. A trained tidewright may stabilize a damaged anchor for several minutes using intact copper, brine, and the correct pattern, at serious fatigue; this buys time for ordinary hauling and masonry. A large repair needs several trained practitioners over days, material replacement, and safe tidal windows. The mechanism cannot function indefinitely on one person's power. Cael's ward can clarify echoes for workers but does not substitute for tide training.

Opening the wrong shutter sends seawater into natural fissures toward the low shore east of Rookharbour. It can flood gardens and undermine the eastern wall foundations over repeated high tides. It cannot suddenly drown the entire town from sixteen kilometres away. Closing both sea intakes at low water and physically blocking their damaged channels is a viable permanent shutdown. That demands hauling and bracing under unstable masonry; it sacrifices the lower dry chambers and makes this refuge unusable. Removing a brace under load can kill anyone in the chamber. Tide timing, the physical route to each wheel, and a retreat path must matter in any story using the site.

## Yseld before the story

Yseld is a formerly human vampire who was an apprentice tidewright before being turned in 213 IS at twenty-nine. She has lived long enough to learn patience and cruelty, not to acquire a new category of power. She survived the Three Storms in these chambers, then lost the safest rooms as the anchor beds worsened. She now moves between an opaque service crypt and a dry loft above the old kitchen. She knows the Sill's operating patterns but is neither an Ilyran master nor able to repair it alone.

Her declared ambition is a secure refuge where vampires need not flee every dawn. Her chosen means are domination: dependent helpers, secluded donors who cannot leave freely, and reopening dangerous chambers while others bear the flood risk. She believes humans inevitably become hunters once they learn enough. That belief explains her choices without making them inevitable or correct. She has fed through intimidation and has injured people who resisted. She may offer real protection from other threats and still be the threat a protected person most needs to escape.

Her existing blood access is dispersed and fragile. Over six years she has approached roughly two dozen adult fishers, itinerant workers, and isolated household members around the corridor. Some accepted a payment or protection before understanding the danger; most continuing access now depends on threats. She takes from an individual no more often than roughly every nine to twelve weeks when her preferred rotation holds. At that spacing, about two dozen available people can meet one vampire's need, provided they remain healthy and reachable. They are not all imprisoned together, and their silence is neither perfect nor assured. Several have fled, fallen ill, or begun avoiding the arranged meeting places. Yseld sometimes uses a single night of animal blood to bridge a failed meeting, and hunts for replacements when access breaks down. This is exploitation that happens to permit recovery intervals, not consent or a safe community system. Adding a newly turned vampire immediately makes her existing supply inadequate; two people cannot draw indefinitely from the same frightened households.

Yseld seeks adults with local route knowledge, practical hands, and people they fear losing. Turning an injured person can create dependence on her knowledge and access to blood, but produces no mystical obedience. She cannot hear Mara at a distance, issue irresistible commands, read memories, or recognize a lie by supernatural instinct. Coercion must work through actions and circumstances. Her wish for a home gives the region a durable antagonist motive; it does not predetermine how Mara encounters her or how any story ends.

## Seasons and other lives available here

Late autumn brings short working days for humans, long hunting darkness for vampires, apple pressing, wet paths, and the year's most dangerous tide-and-storm combinations. Winter brings hunger and roof damage more often than deep snow. Spring raises the brook and opens migration routes for fish; summer daylight makes vampire travel especially difficult while fruit work fills otherwise quiet lanes. No season is uniformly favourable to a turned person.

Independent stories can follow a Briarbridge charcoal family resisting a forest fire order, a rescue crew searching an overturned pilgrim boat, siblings disagreeing about leaving for Velisara, a mason deciding whether a damaged sea wall is safe to work beneath, or Cael's congregation divided over sanctuary. The Listening Sill is one local danger among these lives. A story need not use it, and its closure would not settle the region's forest conflict, bereavements, ambitions, or politics.
