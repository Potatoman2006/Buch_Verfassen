# The Velisaran Republic

Velisara climbs around western bays in terraces of pale stone, red roofs, sailcloth awnings, and citrus trees trained against warm walls. Its harbours face the routes between the Inner Salt and the wider ocean. Music reaches the water from open courtyards; so do arguments, cooking smells, and the hammering of shipyards. The republic's reputation for knowing distant things is deserved. Its belief that knowing something means understanding it is less reliable.

## Quay lives and hill lives

The lower city of Vela gathers shipwrights, pilots, translators, hostel keepers, migrant families, and sailors waiting on wind. Thalassic and Merennic mingle with Low Meridian. Serev riggers, human mariners, Ruun cooks, Aelri map painters, and Oshari visitors at the wet quays are visible parts of ordinary life. Arrival does not guarantee welcome: a celebrated foreign singer and an unemployed foreign deckhand can encounter different republics on the same street.

In the sheltered hills, villages grow olives, beans, vines, and citrus, collect water carefully, and send younger relatives to the port. Families eat late under vines and repair the same courtyard oven through generations. Their residents accuse the city of mistaking every village for a pleasant view. City households reply that ships bring the grain the rocky hills cannot grow. Both depend on the other's work and enjoy pretending they do not.

Velisarans mark departures with a meal at which each traveller chooses one familiar dish. On return, that dish is served again if the household can obtain it. Its absence can announce a poor harvest or a dead cook without any formal speech. Adoption by a ship household is common, but the captain's practical authority at sea can become oppressive when carried into life ashore.

## A republic with old houses

Public assemblies choose senior magistrates for short terms, while a handful of old maritime houses own ships, train navigators, and arrange marriages across generations. House Vesca is especially influential in the western routes. Its leading daughter, Adra, wants a stronger fleet to protect ships near the damaged straits. Rival house leaders suspect a private dominion dressed as public defence. Sailors whose friends vanished along those routes care less about constitutional purity than whether anyone will escort them.

A more intimate conflict divides the city over the Returnless, sailors and passengers missing since the Three Storms. Some families want renewed searches along dangerous outer islands. Others insist that admitting death is necessary to remarry, move house, or stop spending each spring on another expedition. Reports of survivors circulate because survival is possible, not because every hopeful report is true. Opportunists sell reassurance; genuine witnesses are mistrusted after earlier frauds.

## Faith facing outward

The Choir of Tides holds dawn meals before major departures. Lamp shelters receive survivors regardless of which house employed their ship. Dawn Path observers keep hilltop gardens where children learn the moon's motion and the difference between a cloud bank and a coastline. These institutions cooperate over rescue and quarrel over whether a traveller is obliged to return to family once danger has passed.

The Lantern Below is a local belief that a light burns beneath the western straits for everyone who dies beyond familiar shores. No author fact confirms a divine lamp. Divers do observe patches of cold marine light and fragments of Ilyran material in deep water. Priests who explain those phenomena as the sacred may be sincere without knowing the origin of either.

## Navigation, illusion, and the sea's size

Velisaran chart painters use patient observation, conversations with pilots, and accumulated mistakes. Perception craft can project an existing chart through a prepared glass surface so a class can examine it together. It cannot reveal an unvisited coastline. The greatest temptation is to let a beautiful projection make an uncertain report look measured. Good teachers deliberately leave unresolved areas blank.

The Saffron Steps are a drowned Ilyran stair exposed in pieces at exceptional low water. Their surface sometimes carries the sensory impression of sunlight at an angle the present sky does not match. The effect is local remembered light, incapable of warming a body or protecting a vampire from actual daylight. Explorers go there for carvings, sheltering shellfish, or the pleasure of walking a vanished shore. Some do not return before the tide.

Sailbacks pass offshore during their seasonal movement between cooler feeding grounds and warmer breeding water. Their great backs can be mistaken for low islands until they rise to breathe. Velisaran captains argue about whether ships should yield particular channels during the migration. A collision can kill sailors and an animal neither side intended to threaten.

## Places and entanglements

Vela's House of Tongues is a crowded school whose kitchen is as important as its lecture rooms. The Window Gardens overlook a bay where apprentices paint the same headland in different weather until they stop confusing beauty with a navigable description. Outer Quay has wet steps and shaded pools suited to Oshari visitors, beside hostels whose owners compete over the quality of their return meals. The Saffron Steps lie outside safe everyday harbour traffic.

Merenne supplies fruit, timber, and people who often remain in the republic. Seven Boughs resists demands for large old trees; shipbuilders must choose between waiting, redesigning hulls, or encouraging theft. Greyhaven competes for crews while sharing news of storms. Nine Beacons pilots guide unfamiliar deep-water captains through the southern passages. The wider ocean brings visitors whose homeland does not appear anywhere on the regional chart.

## Open stories

A navigator returns after years of absence to a spouse who has built a good life without them. A hill village sends a reluctant delegate to stop its spring being diverted toward a fashionable garden. A ship household rescues someone its captain once abandoned. Two translators disagree over the meaning of a foreign funeral request while the bereaved wait. A migration of sailbacks closes the only safe passage for a desperate rescue expedition, forcing a choice no shipowner can make from shore.
