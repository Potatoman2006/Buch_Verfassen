# The Redwind Khanates

Redwind begins east of the Crownspine, where the air dries, grass carries the sound of approaching hooves, and evening makes distant ridges look close enough to touch. Its khanates join moving households, settled winter towns, pasture circuits, and desert-edge sanctuaries. A border drawn across the grass is a claim about one season. The next season may place the same family somewhere a foreign map calls another country.

## Tents, orchards, and winter streets

The western Aruk circuits combine human, Urven, and Kharad herding households. Farther east, the Othuun lake towns hold Serev weavers, human gardeners, and Aelri families descended from much older migrations. Sarayi is widespread, with distinct dialects and borrowed Vornic around the mountain markets. People move for marriage, pasture, trade, study, or exile; riding skill is learned rather than inherited through a people's blood.

Mobile homes use felt, timber frames, and furnishings chosen for repeated packing. Household labour includes milking, weaving, cooking, childbirth, mending, watching young animals, and caring for elders. Those who do not ride far still sustain those who do. Winter towns grow fruit and vegetables beside protected water and host noisy competitions of poetry, wrestling, and horsemanship. A guest receives tea before being asked the reason for arrival. Refusing the second cup can mean hurry, grief, or dislike, depending on the household.

## A succession carried on horseback

Khan Aruk Temur is dying slowly from an ordinary wasting illness. His daughter Saran has kept western households together through drought by bargaining for mountain access. His nephew Vek seeks a great eastern campaign, promising new pasture and the restoration of prestige. Saran considers his promised enemy a convenient collection of neighbours. Vek believes her compromises will leave Redwind dependent on Kharad gates and Greyhaven markets. Temur has refused to choose publicly, and every visit to his tent becomes a contest disguised as affection.

Three shortened grazing circuits now approach the same western valleys. A movement called the Unfenced Wells demands emergency access. Settled gardeners fear losing crops if thousands of animals drink at once. Neither a romantic right to roam nor an absolute right to exclude can supply water that is absent. Armed followers of both heirs have begun escorting herds, bringing a succession dispute into households trying to keep animals alive.

## Open Sky and the buried road

Open Sky rites honour the ability to depart and the obligation to receive someone who arrives exhausted. At a funeral, mourners ride or walk away from the grave in several directions before returning to the household. No path is declared the one the dead must follow. Town congregations combine this practice with Lamp shelters and ancestor meals. A fixed home does not disqualify someone from the faith.

The buried Road of Kings crosses eastern gravel country. After rare rain, stone horses emerge from the sand in ranks, some broken, some carrying riders with unfamiliar faces. They belonged to an old dynasty that preceded the present khanates. Local families guide pilgrims there and disagree over whether the figures honour rulers or the people those rulers sacrificed. Occasional hoofbeat echoes remain tied to the exposed stone. The uncertainty of intention leaves the place powerful without promising a resurrected army.

## The red migration

Every few years, enormous herds of dust-horned grazers move west from beyond the chart. They are living animals with routes older than the current khanates. Their passing can flatten crops, replenish predators, and transform the availability of food. This year's first scouts have seen unusually early columns. Vek wants to drive the migration toward an enemy's settled fields. Saran fears frightened animals will break west into already crowded refuge valleys.

Redwind outriders use cloth screens, smoke, calls, and careful positioning. A few perception workers add short-lived false shapes through prepared mirrors and dust-coloured veils, confusing a herd's leading edge. They cannot command thousands of animals. A failed attempt can turn an intended manoeuvre into a stampede that kills those who planned it. Hearth drakes follow the migration's sick and dead, gathering around night camps where scraps are easy to find.

## Places, relations, and open stories

Aruk's winter town, Serekh, holds bathhouses around warm springs and a market of embroidered felt bright against snow. Othuun's reed gardens surround saline lakes with fresh-water margins. The Road of Kings lies beyond reliable everyday wells. At the White Saddle, families meet mountain relatives beneath stones hung with wind-torn cloth.

Kharad gates offer refuge and provoke resentment when closed. Greyhaven needs animals and then distrusts the families accompanying them. Avarenne's eastern nobles share riding customs while competing over pasture and prestige. An heir's marriage could join several households; a raid could make that same marriage impossible.

A non-riding daughter must carry her household through a succession without becoming anyone's symbol. A scout discovers the migration has changed course and must cross rival camps to warn her kin. A gardener shelters an enemy heir during fever. Two estranged brothers guide pilgrims to the stone horses, where a storm strips away the safe return route. A young khan wins the loyalty he wanted and learns what followers now expect him to do to keep it.
