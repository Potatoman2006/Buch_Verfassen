# The Inner Salt

The Inner Salt is a partly enclosed sea in the Greater Meridian, bordered by twelve named political regions and countless smaller places they do not fully govern. The regional chart covers approximately 4,800 by 3,200 kilometres. A traveller can spend a lifetime within it and still regard half its shores as distant country. Beyond those shores, other seas, continental interiors, and civilisations continue. This module connects the regions without pretending that a coloured border explains a landscape.

## Reading the basin

The western entrance bends through dangerous straits between the Merenne–Velisara shore and southern islands. Wet ocean weather strikes the western forests and headlands first. Merenne's orchard valleys and Seven Boughs' watersheds rise inland from that moisture. Farther north, Rhydder's high roads approach the Crownspine. Kharad-Vorn occupies the mountain gates and valleys; snow and rock separate settlements that appear close together on the schematic map.

The Lume descends through the northern and central lowlands, linking Avarenne's grain country with Orison's shifting rivers. Greyhaven's nine cities face the cooler northern reaches of the sea. Beyond the Crownspine's rain shadow, the Redwind grasslands extend farther east than this chart follows. The southern basin holds the Aurelian Isles and Nine Beacons, with the Reef Crown's inhabited shallow waters around warm island shelves. Velisara occupies a western maritime hinge, reaching more places by ship than its land area would suggest.

These placements describe broad relationships, not perfect contiguous rectangles. Enclaves, empty marsh, alpine pasture, mixed villages, and disputed shorelines interrupt every border. A Seven Bough family can live under Merenne's duke; a Greyhaven ship can belong to an Aelri captain whose grandparents came from Redwind. River mouth, mountain pass, and sailing season often explain loyalties better than ancestry.

## Movement has a human scale

An ordinary traveller walks twenty to thirty kilometres on a good day with usable paths and food. A merchant cart is slower on hills; a messenger changing horses is faster and much more expensive to sustain. Coastal ships commonly cover eighty to one hundred sixty kilometres in a sailing day when weather serves. They also spend days waiting, loading, repairing, or refusing an unsafe departure. Crossing the basin is a journey of weeks or months, not a succession of adjoining city scenes.

The most travelled northern chain runs from Merenne's ports toward the Lume, then upriver through Orison and Avarenne toward Crownspine approaches. The eastern chain carries Kharad metal and Redwind livestock toward Greyhaven. Southern traffic travels through pilot grounds, island water stops, and reef passages. A ship carrying grain cannot casually take the same shallow route as an Oshari canoe. Maps show possible connections; pilots know which ones are possible this month.

Languages travel unevenly. Low Meridian helps strangers buy supper, hire a room, or ask where a road leads. It does not make a local funeral intelligible or an intricate magical instruction safe. Merennic, Lumic, Rhyd, Vornic, Sarayi, Bough tongues, and Thalassic each carry memories that translators can explain but cannot compress into one equivalent word. A character who has worked three ports may know three useful vocabularies and still be unable to read any of them.

## The inherited wound

The Ilyran dominion left roads, culverts, tide chambers, terraces, and monuments across this basin. Its works were unevenly distributed and built through ordinary labour alongside learned magic. The Saltfall of year 0 broke the Great Sill at the western connection, drowned old shores, and scarred the overlap between living matter and remembered form. The present coast is an inheritance of that disaster and subsequent erosion. It is not an empire waiting intact beneath the sea.

Some old sites remain useful; some have become dangerous; many are simply stones reclaimed by plants. An echo can repeat the sound of a marketplace that no longer exists without revealing its inhabitants' secrets. A stone anchor can ease strain at a gate without moving a mountain. Current powers compete over working sites because a maintained landing or pass is valuable, not because every ruin contains a world-ending weapon. Archaeology can still be perilous: water, unstable masonry, local resentment, and partial instructions are sufficient reasons.

## Present tensions cross borders

The Three Storms of 244 damaged western sea walls and southern lights. Merenne now wants long timbers from Seven Boughs. Foresters fear that cutting the oldest wet slopes will send mud into downstream villages. Velisaran shipyards also want timber to replace lost hulls. Each demand is understandable; meeting all three with the same grove is impossible. A rescue mission, a timber raid, or a marriage across that frontier can enter the same problem through different lives.

Snow failure in the eastern Crownspine has shortened several Redwind grazing circuits. Herds gathering near Kharad gates bring food and animals, but also pressure on meadow and water. Greyhaven wants dependable eastern supply while its poorer city districts already resent imported families. Avarenne's queen could ease shortages with grain, yet remembers the naval retaliation in the War of the Two Harvests. Orison remembers whose river villages suffered when that conflict moved inland.

Farther south, warmer currents are changing the Reef Crown's nurseries. Nine Beacons pilots must learn altered passages; Aurelian observers disagree about which changes are seasonal and which endure. Coastal landholders sometimes call the movement of Oshari settlements an invasion even when it follows a moving food source. Southern instability reaches the north through missing fish, sick crews, delayed medicines, and sailors whose families are no longer where their last letter described.

## What is shared and what remains distinct

Every shore knows the fear of a failed harvest and the relief of a returned boat. Shared festivals nonetheless carry different meanings. A floating lamp can honour a rescuer in Merenne, warn the dead away from a Greyhaven hearth, or welcome an expected traveller in the Nine Beacons. These are related practices, not proof of one secret universal religion. Divine existence and intention remain questions of faith; functioning magic proves particular learned effects.

Peoples are not regional uniforms. A Kharad child raised in a Redwind household may speak Sarayi, ride well, and dislike underground spaces. An Urven scholar can serve an Aurelian observatory. Oshari cold-blooded physiology shapes shelter, work, and illness without deciding political sympathy. Vampirism can afflict sapient warm-blooded adults of different peoples and remains a condition, never a thirteenth nation.

## Stories the region can carry

A war narrative can follow the people who must cross a pass before winter, while a family story stays in the village providing their mules. Exploration can mean finding the route around a changed reef rather than discovering an empty continent. A religious story can ask whether a sanctuary should shelter a dangerous fugitive whose hunger cannot be cured. Political stories can turn on succession, conquest, refugees, competing historical memories, or the cost of protecting one shore by damaging another.

The available scale ranges from one room to an ocean voyage. The world does not require every story to tour its twelve regions. Choosing a bounded local corridor should reveal a portion of larger life: a foreign lullaby, an imported tool, a pilgrim's difficult accent, a war that explains an older person's silence. Detailed local consequences create vastness more reliably than a catalogue of distant kings.
