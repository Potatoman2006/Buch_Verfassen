# Lamps, roots and the listening dark

Religion enters a life through small acts before it becomes an argument about the heavens: a lamp set in a sickroom, bread broken above a grave, a sailor touching the harbour water before departure. The Inner Salt contains overlapping faiths with real houses, teachers, festivals and divisions. Practitioners sometimes produce demonstrable magical effects. Those effects establish that the working happened; the existence and intentions of particular gods remain matters of faith.

## The Covenant of Lamps

The Covenant teaches that every living person carries a light for which others can make shelter. Its central divine figure is the Keeper, imagined as a woman with soot on her hands, a hooded traveller or a presence without a body. House lamps use ordinary oil. At the Feast of Returning Light, neighbours carry flame between houses and remember people who failed to come home.

Saint Orra's example gives the tradition its strongest practical demand: warm and tend the injured before questioning them. Hospices, travelling healers and burial houses follow it with varying success. The **Open-Hand houses** extend care to enemies and to vampires willing to refrain from predation. The **Pure-Flame preachers** believe shelter becomes complicity when it preserves a dangerous appetite. Both remember massacres and rescued children. Their quarrel cuts through individual congregations and families.

Burial commonly places a clay lamp beside the dead, its wick removed. The household keeps burning the living lamp. This signifies the distinction between remembering somebody and asking them to remain. [Saint Orra's](../regions/story-ground.md) belongs to this inheritance even where its roof has failed.

## Root Communion

Root Communion finds sacred relationship in the exchange among soil, water, plants, animals and the dead. The **Many-Root** is described as a divine presence, a vast living kinship or a truth too large to resemble a person. Worshippers return selected remains to burial groves and plant different species together so that no single tree becomes the sole bearer of a family's grief.

Spring rites include cleaning streams and a shared meal eaten before the first work begins. The **Closed Grove** tradition protects old sacred places through restricted entry. **Hearth-Root** communities hold that stewardship requires inhabited, changing landscapes and welcome new households into ritual. Disputes concern necessary felling, burial, refugees and who may speak for beings that cannot argue in a council.

Roots sometimes grow into exposed wounds and repeat older patterns of branching. Communion keepers study such groves carefully. Their holiness is a belief; the danger of roots breaking a sleeping house is physical.

## Forge Ancestors

Forge Ancestor worship honours the dead through useful work. At a first firing, a workshop places a small imperfect object in the furnace: something honestly made by the newest learner. The offering acknowledges that every accomplished hand was once clumsy. The sacred **First Smith** is variously a divine maker, the first mortal to share fire, or a name for generations of forgotten teachers.

One tradition keeps old tools working through repeated repair. Another retires a master's last tools so that younger hands must discover their own balance. In some halls, these become fierce arguments about authority. A beautiful ancestral hammer can inspire an apprentice or justify denying them a better design.

Funerary songs name acts of care alongside famous works. The woman who kept a household warm during a siege may receive a longer verse than the cousin who forged a king's sword.

## The Deep Covenant

The Deep Covenant attends to darkness, silence and the spaces that support inhabited stone. Its devotees speak of the **Listener Below**, who receives everything that falls beyond human hearing. A traditional service begins with every flame extinguished for nine slow breaths. The rite teaches attention and dependence: somebody must relight the room.

**Threshold keepers** tend safe passages, refuge chambers and places where echoes trouble the living. **Descent seekers** pursue revelation in deep fissures and sometimes mistake disorientation for revelation. Their expeditions attract the grieving and the ambitious as well as the devout. Forge worship and the Deep Covenant often share a household, though some followers distrust each other's confidence in making or unmaking.

The faith does not possess a reliable road to an afterlife. Voices heard below may be air, fear or actual remembered sound. Wise keepers learn the difference before leading children into the dark.

## The Choir of Tides

The Choir gives the sea several divine names, sung in sequence because no single mood encompasses it. Worship combines working songs, rescue practice and mourning. At an empty burial, relatives lower a stone wrapped in the missing person's clothing into a sheltered pool, then share a meal with the rescuers who returned without them.

The Nine Fires of 73 IS are its great shared exemplar. **Harbour choirs** stress warning lights, shelter and preparation. **Blue-Water congregations** emphasise courage, voyaging and accepting the sea's indifference. Their arguments often begin after a rescue fails: how much may a community demand from those capable of going back out?

Tideworkers sometimes sing while practising magic because measured breath holds a pattern steady. A beautiful hymn by itself changes no current. Worshippers know this as clearly as they know that a song can steady frightened hands.

## Open Sky

Open Sky traditions honour the space through which living things move and the obligations created by sharing it. Many address **Asha of the Far Wind**, pictured with an open hand and no throne. On receiving a guest, a host pours the first cup of water onto bare earth, acknowledging needs beyond the people presently seated.

Seasonal gatherings combine marriage introductions, horse races, storytelling and remembrance of those who died between camps. Some teachers defend unrestricted emergency refuge at wells; others insist that hospitality cannot survive the destruction of the households expected to provide it. Settled followers plant shade trees beside roads and dispute travelling preachers who treat staying in one place as spiritual failure.

## The Dawn Path

The Dawn Path holds that attention is a form of reverence. Its image of the **Unblinking Dawn** appears in observatories, gardens and modest rooms where an elder teaches a child to draw a leaf accurately. Worshippers greet sunrise by naming something they misunderstood the previous day.

The **Clear Horizon** schools favour public demonstration and patient correction. **Hidden Sun** circles cultivate inward discipline, dreams and private revelation. Both have produced generous teachers and domineering masters. Aurelian prestige has made the tradition fashionable abroad, occasionally reducing a demanding spiritual practice to elegant clothing and expensive instruments.

## The witnesses and the remembered dead

The **Nine Witnesses** of Orison are divine figures said to have watched the first dawn from nine places and described it differently. Their followers seat an empty chair at important communal meals for the person whose experience nobody present can supply. River shrines hang nine differently coloured panes around one lamp. The light becomes many colours without ceasing to be one flame. One school seeks reconciliation among accounts; another teaches that some disagreements must remain beside each other without forced resolution.

Rhydder's **Roadside Witnesses** are a related but independent tradition of local guardians and remembered travellers. Their wayside stones receive water, flowers and small carvings of animals that helped a journey. Greyhaven's **Dusk Memorial Houses** welcome people remembering the dead across several faiths. Mourners speak the dead person's name, then describe a small ordinary act rather than a heroic achievement. Some houses invite echo workers to share retained sounds; others refuse, fearing a familiar voice will keep grief fixed around one moment.

## Local gods and shared tables

Village saints, hearth spirits and hill guardians fill the space between these larger traditions. A family can follow Lamp burial, sing a sea prayer and leave flowers for a local spring without experiencing a contradiction. Religious institutions differ about such combinations. Some welcome them; others seek a purity that ordinary life keeps frustrating.

Faith can sustain courage when practical knowledge runs out. It can also grant cruelty a noble name. Holy symbols alone do not burn vampires or repel other creatures. An effective ward uses the same material magic available outside religion. A healer's mercy remains consequential even when the lamp beside the bed is entirely ordinary.
