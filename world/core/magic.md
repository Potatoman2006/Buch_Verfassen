# The undercurrent and the six crafts

A stone remembers pressure; a root follows forms older than the tree; water enters the hollow it has entered a thousand times. Beneath the living world's present lies an undercurrent of remembered form. Magic brings a prepared piece of the present into contact with an attainable pattern in that undercurrent. Practitioners work through substances, learned movements, measured sound and careful attention. Their successes are tangible: a cracked pier holds through a storm, an orchard survives frost, a wounded person lives long enough to heal.

## The foundation

Every working needs **a material medium, a learned pattern and sustained effort**. A craft redirects an existing physical process or reveals a limited remembered impression. It cannot create souls, restore a dead person, reverse time, teleport, command minds or solve hunger. Food, fuel, water and other materials remain necessary. A useful intervention often exchanges one immediate danger for a manageable burden: drawing heat away from a patient warms something nearby; hastening a plant's repair consumes its stored nourishment.

Anyone among the eight peoples may learn, though aptitude differs and disability, illness or temperament can make one approach easier than another. Fluency in one craft provides little automatic command of another. Reliable household work takes years of teaching and practice. The finest practitioners still need tools, supplies and other people. Vampires can practise learned magic under these same limits; turning gives no new talent.

Ordinary work happens within reach or within a few metres of a prepared medium. Distance weakens control. Walls, broken lines of material, motion and distraction interfere according to the craft. Greater effects require a functioning site: shaped stone, channels, beds, furnaces or other connected structures maintained over time. A lone person cannot point at a distant fortress and redirect its foundations.

## Ember craft: heat and its passage

Ember workers use charcoal, prepared ceramics, metal and controlled breathing to guide existing heat. A smith may even out the temperature across a blade; a healer may move dangerous local heat into warmed compresses that must be replaced; a cook may keep a small oven steady while its fuel burns. The craft cannot provide heat from nothing. Cooling one place requires somewhere for that heat to go.

Kharad furnace schools teach rhythm through hammer taps and ceramic beads. Aurelian practitioners favour glazed plates whose colour reveals changing temperature. Both methods can work. Damp fuel, cracked vessels and a disrupted pattern cause hot spots, scalding or a sudden return of heat. The worker risks dehydration, trembling and loss of concentration.

Battlefield use favours prepared firepots, furnaces and defended positions. Directly boiling a moving person's blood is beyond the craft: the body is an enclosed, changing system with no safely established medium. Fire remains dangerous enough when somebody can aim an ordinary burning projectile accurately.

## Green craft: growth and repair

Green workers use living tissue, moisture, mineral preparations and patterned touch. They can guide a graft, encourage clotting, help a wound close cleanly or draw roots toward water already present in the soil. They cannot make a plant grow without nourishment or replace organs whose structure has been destroyed. A person kept alive by magic still needs food, rest, clean dressings and time.

Seven Boughs teaches through gardens whose students inherit earlier mistakes. Lamp houses teach the body alongside washing, bandaging and observation. Poor practice can seal dirt inside a wound or exhaust a tree's reserves for one impressive flowering. A rapid recovery may leave fever and profound hunger behind it. The worker also tires, and repeated attempts become less precise.

Green craft cannot cure vampirism: the condition continually sustains a different bodily pattern. It can assist an ordinary wound's treatment only within the vampire's own slow recovery requirements. No treatment removes the need for blood or shelter from daylight.

## Stone song: stress, joining and fracture

Stone singers use mineral dust, fitted stones, resonant tools and repeated sound to sense and redirect stress along prepared surfaces. They can discover weaknesses, encourage a fitted repair to settle, or loosen a small damaged section along an existing fracture. Quarry workers use the craft to save labour; builders use it to avoid losing a bridge.

The sound is physical, usually low and repetitive. Ear protection, rest and careful staging matter. Bad work may conceal a dangerous strain until an apparently sound wall fails. Dense foreign inclusions, water-filled cracks and unexpected foundations complicate the pattern. Stone cannot be made weightless or shaped like wet clay by a casual gesture.

Large mountain works join several singers with carpenters, masons and people listening at distant points. Breaking a tuning rod or removing a necessary wedge can interrupt a current working. It does not automatically reverse a collapse already begun. Existing loads continue acting after magic stops.

## Tide craft: flow through a prepared way

Tideworkers use water, saline mineral mixtures, marked basins and measured pulses to bias flow along an existing connected route. A harbour crew can ease a surge through maintained channels or assist a gate at a difficult moment. A small working may steady water around a mooring briefly. The craft cannot summon a storm, command the open sea or move a ship against arbitrary distance and weather.

Ilyran sites multiplied the effect through extensive physical channels and chambers. A functioning complex may influence water hundreds of metres along those connected works. Restoring a large broken system needs several practitioners, repair crews and time; a former expert can briefly operate an intact small gate alone, at considerable personal cost. The architecture supplies the reach. A bowl of seawater does not.

Pressure shifted from one chamber must go somewhere. Neglected outlets can flood a neighbouring cellar or tear a weakened bank. Salt concentration, tide phase and debris alter performance. Workers suffer nausea, shaking and impaired balance. A severed feed channel or displaced calibration stone can stop deliberate control while leaving moving water and stored pressure dangerously active.

## Veil craft: the path of perception

Veil workers prepare pigments, fine smoke, polished surfaces or stretched translucent cloth to alter light and sound crossing a small space. They can soften a silhouette, hide a stationary doorway from a particular angle, bend a reflected image or muffle a room's ordinary sounds. They cannot enter thoughts, implant desires or force belief. A person may be deceived by what reaches their senses and remain entirely free to doubt it.

The best practitioners understand lighting, architecture and how people look. A moving subject is harder to conceal than a still surface. Rain washes pigment, wind scatters smoke, touch exposes a false opening and a second viewpoint may reveal the trick. A veil is not perfect invisibility. Performance traditions use the craft openly for theatre; thieves and soldiers use related techniques with different intentions.

Headache, afterimages and loss of depth judgement follow prolonged work. Attempting to sustain a complex veil while fighting or fleeing usually breaks it. Opaque physical shelter, rather than a perceptual veil, protects a vampire from sunlight.

## Echo craft: what remains of an event

Echo workers use objects or places that retained a strong impression, with tuned vessels, ash, stone dust or resonant wire as media. They may make a limited sensory fragment audible or visible: several footsteps, a burst of singing, the colour of a sleeve passing a doorway. The result is fixed and partial. It cannot answer questions, reveal thoughts or become the living consciousness of a dead person.

Repeated events blur together. A familiar room might hold a muddle of meals rather than its most recent murder. Violent events often leave intense impressions but lack the context that would explain them. Observers can misidentify a voice or infer an intention that was never recorded. Echo craft offers experience, not infallible testimony.

At wounded sites, impressions can intrude without invitation. A **Drowned Choir** is a persistent tangle of remembered voices and movements associated with submerged habitation. It may obscure present sight and sound, causing panic or accidental injury. Its figures cannot physically seize somebody. Echo workers can damp an exposed local pattern through appropriate media and repair, but destroying its surrounding structure may expose further impressions instead.

## Workings that change an encounter

The **Red Heron** is an ember fighter's art: oil-fed clay chambers along a short lance release a sudden fan of flame when its prepared heat pattern is opened. Its brightness fills a room, ignites hanging fabric and makes an armoured advance falter. The fighter carries only a few charges; a cracked chamber can burn the hand holding it. Ember swords use a related fuel-bearing sheath to heat an edge for a brief exchange, leaving a weapon that must be cooled and examined before another attempt.

The **Widow's Curtain** draws a prepared living hedge across a narrow path. Green workers guide existing pliant growth, harden thorns and spend the hedge's stored resources to close the last gaps. Boots and cloaks catch as pursuers arrive. A well-grown barrier can hold long after its maker leaves; the hedge may subsequently lose a season's fruit or die back. Forest defenders establish such positions in advance, and an enemy with axes and time can open them again.

**Stonebreak** requires the singer's hand or tool on a mineral surface whose weakness they can feel. A practised stroke drives a local fracture open: a damaged stair collapses, a stone-faced shield splits, or a wedge of cliff breaks beneath a pursuer. The singer must approach, read the material and choose the moment. Their ally standing on the same stair shares the danger.

Veil theatres produce **the White Procession**, a train of luminous stags moving through prepared smoke and reflected light. Antlers seem to pass through spectators' shoulders. At war the same materials can stretch a corridor into apparent branching passages or briefly duplicate an advancing line. The spectacle crosses eyes and ears, not thoughts; movement through it reveals empty space.

Echo fighters carry **mourning masks**, copper resonators with wet ceramic cartridges prepared at sites retaining violent sound. Opened together, several masks fill a short stretch of battlefield, around twenty metres across, with overlapping remembered screams and weapon impacts. A cartridge lasts minutes, and the wearers hear it too. Trained crews use familiar rhythms to find one another inside the confusion. At an uncontrolled Drowned Choir, a person may hear their own present cry vanish beneath hundreds of older cries while an unseen rising tide reaches their knees.

## Wards, wounds and ancient works

A ward is a maintained application of one or more crafts. Ember wards warm a threshold to dangerous temperatures; veil wards conceal an entrance; an echo arrangement may replay a warning when physical contact disturbs its prepared medium. The arrangement has a trigger, material supply and a bounded effect. A painted symbol without such preparation is a symbol.

Wounds are sites where remembered form presses unusually strongly against the present. Some predate known settlement. Others followed the Saltfall's catastrophic physical and magical failures. Their effects remain tied to place and material: repeating heat, abnormal growth, dangerous flows or sensory fragments. A wound does not grant arbitrary powers to whoever needs them.

Ancient complexes survive through stonework and maintenance as well as magic. Their scale can be magnificent: long black galleries humming beneath a city, terraces flowering out of season over heated rock, tide chambers whose rising water fills a cathedral-sized void. Their vulnerability is equally material. A storm, missing worker or neglected outlet can undo what generations held together.
