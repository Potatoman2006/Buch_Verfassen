# The living country and its hungers

At the edge of Seven Boughs, antlers ring together somewhere beyond the firelight. In Crownspine, moth colonies turn whole caverns into trembling silver. Far out in the southern channels, a line of dark backs rises through the sea and every captain watching changes course. Aetherra's living world includes familiar crops and animals alongside creatures and habitats shaped by deep time and the undercurrent. Their movements alter roads, harvests and wars.

## Fields, waters and seasons

Wet western weather feeds orchards, beech woods, pastures and dark productive soils. Merenne grows apples, pears, beans and hardy grains; Orison's floodplain supports reeds, vegetables and fish; Avarenne's broad northern river country produces much of the region's grain. Crownspine valleys offer short growing seasons and summer pasture. Eastward, Redwind's grasslands depend on rain, snow-fed streams and careful seasonal movement. Southern islands grow olives, citrus, pulses and warm-weather grains where freshwater permits.

Households preserve food by drying, salting, smoking, fermenting and keeping it cool. Magical care can save a threatened graft or moderate a frost pocket, but fields still need soil, labour and water. Removing forest changes runoff downstream; killing marsh predators lets grazing animals destroy reed beds; blocking a fish migration can impoverish settlements that never see the dam.

Autumn storms, winter passes, spring floods and summer drought shape travel. The same route can be a road, a muddy trap or flowing water at different seasons. Seasonal abundance supports gatherings and expeditions; scarcity sends people home, toward markets or into conflict.

## The Briar-Wake

The **Briar-Wake** is a living growth front in and around Seven Boughs, rooted in damaged Ilyran cultivation beds. Its thorn thickets rise over cleared ground in dense, interwoven walls, sometimes advancing several metres in a wet week. New growth draws heavily on water and soil nutrients. Behind a vigorous advance, ordinary plants yellow and springs may weaken. In dry conditions the front slows, hardens and waits.

At night, split seed husks click through the thorn walls like teeth. Branches trap fallen animals and root through their remains. The forest's bell stags sometimes carry thorn seeds tangled in their antlers, spreading the growth across cleared roads. A settlement that burns one approach may discover green shoots along the stream behind it.

The Briar-Wake is real and ongoing. People amplify or restrain it using the established green craft; the Thorn Court's use of rooted stakes turns an ecological danger into a weapon. Clearing requires sustained cutting, removal of active roots and restoration of exhausted ground. Fire alone can leave the prepared beds intact. Routes lost to the Wake change marriages, trade and the ability to send help during a raid.

## Bell stags and the scarwood edge

Bell stags are large deer whose hollow antler channels produce low ringing tones in wind or during clashes. In rutting season, an entire valley can sound as if distant metal is being struck. Their passage announces changing pasture and draws hunters, scavengers and pilgrims who regard the sound as sacred.

Near growth wounds, a rare parasitic thorn establishes itself around damaged antler velvet. It can spread into the animal's skin, drawing nourishment and producing the terrible silhouette called a **scarwood stag**. These animals suffer, become unpredictable and carry viable seed. They are neither human beings transformed nor messengers that can be questioned. A wounded stag charging into a harvest crowd is an immediate danger and a sign that the Briar-Wake has crossed a boundary thought safe.

Forest keepers who track and kill infected animals are sometimes called scarwood riders. Their work divides communities that revere the stags, fear the thorns or suspect the riders serve a hostile faction. Antlers hung above a doorway may commemorate a necessary killing rather than a triumphant hunt.

## The sailback migrations

Sailbacks are enormous marine reptiles, commonly twenty to thirty-five metres long as mature adults, with ridged backs, long tails and jaws capable of breaking a small boat. They breathe air, bear live young and migrate between offshore feeding grounds and sheltered southern breeding channels. Their bodies have no magical immunity. Their mass is sufficient.

Breeding groups close usable passages for weeks. Mothers turn violently toward approaching hulls; a panicked animal can drive through a line of anchored ships. Coastal pilots watch for feeding birds, oily wakes and characteristic broken swells. Fleets timed for war may find their best route occupied by creatures unimpressed by banners.

The scarred old sailback called **White Keel** has returned to the southern approaches for at least forty years. A pale band crosses its back where an old wound healed. Pilots recognise its unusually broad ridge and the pod that follows it. Its seasonal arrival can postpone an invasion, concentrate fishing in safer waters and leave a hungry town arguing whether to attempt a hunt that might destroy half its boats.

## Hearth drakes and the high hunters

Hearth drakes are wingless reptiles ranging from dog to pony size. They shelter near warm rock, kilns and volcanic vents and scavenge eggs, carrion and small animals. Defensive sacs produce a short spray of hot caustic fluid. The discharge is finite and takes days of feeding to replenish. A cornered drake can blind someone or start a fire among dry material; an unwary household may first notice one when its hens vanish.

Crown owls nest on high cliffs and broken towers. Their wide wings carry them quietly over upland pasture; they can kill lambs and small children, though an adult is too heavy to carry away. Their feathers enter ceremonial clothing and specialist fletching. Collecting them from an occupied nest is work for experienced climbers with watchers below.

Reed giants are massive six-limbed marsh herbivores with broad, splayed feet and low, heavily plated heads. Their grazing opens waterways, while their nesting mounds shelter smaller animals. Disturbed adults can crush a boat or a person against a bank. Oshari settlements maintain refuge shelves and escape channels around known breeding grounds. Draining a marsh displaces creatures large enough to destroy the farms meant to replace it.

## Knell-hounds

At certain damp growth wounds, pale fungal colonies knit flexible cords around scavenged bone. Mature colonies form low, jointed hunting shapes known as **knell-hounds**. These are living predatory growths: the old animal's bones provide a frame, while new tissue moves it. Their bodies take months to mature from actual nutrients. They cannot rise instantly from a fresh corpse.

Knell-hounds hunt small animals and vulnerable larger prey, including people. Hollow growths retain fragments of sound from their birthplace; movement forces air through them. A traveller may hear a cough, a broken note of song or the clatter of distant hooves before pale limbs emerge beneath the brush. The sound repeats while the creature moves, terribly indifferent to whatever the listener associates with it.

They depend on damp habitats and return to their growth beds. Several dry days away from moisture disable them. Severing load-bearing cords or breaking limbs stops the corresponding movement; they cannot regenerate during a fight. Fire destroys exposed tissue but is dangerous in occupied woods. Finding one hound means somebody must locate the slower, well-hidden colony from which others will mature.

## Useful wonders

Mineral-fed luminous fungi mark damp galleries in Crownspine. Giant cave moths feed around them and pollinate pale subterranean plants; sudden changes in moth movement warn experienced residents about air currents or disturbance. Some chambers become silver storms when a colony rises together.

Reef blooms build living walls of coloured growth through warm shallows. Their seasonal spawning turns night water luminous enough to show a hand beneath the surface. The spectacle draws visitors while altering channels and fouling nets. Communities protect nursery reaches because beauty, food and safe passage grow from the same living system.
