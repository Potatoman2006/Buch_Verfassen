# The years beneath the present

The Inner Salt has more history than any one people can remember. Aelri witnesses outlive human kingdoms, yet their recollections disagree; Kharad inscriptions survive where their spoken languages do not. Beneath the sea lie houses older than the catastrophe for which the present calendar is named. What follows is the shared historical framework of this edition. Where causes or intentions are uncertain, the uncertainty belongs to the world itself, not to an unfinished chronology.

## Before the Ilyrans

The earliest inhabited remains around the sea are not one lost civilisation. Shore cairns, painted caves, shell roads and deep stone chambers belong to different periods and builders. Some abandoned sites were ordinary settlements made untenable by changing rivers. Some were places of burial. A few stand on wounds older than any surviving account. Nobody should assume that a carved door hides a treasure, or that its makers vanished rather than becoming somebody's grandparents.

Long before the Saltfall, Crownspine households cut high galleries to reach mineral seams. Forest communities cultivated fruiting clearings and moved their dwellings as the canopy changed. Southern islanders navigated by swell and birds. The Lume carried peoples and crops between upland and coast. These societies traded and fought without waiting for an empire to make them historical.

Their surviving legends include the Walking Winter, when glaciers were said to travel overnight, and the Year of the Second Moon, remembered in otherwise unrelated songs. Neither event has a securely established date or agreed natural explanation. This edition does not confirm a second moon or moving glaciers. The shared motifs are evidence of contact, inherited fear or some distant experience, and leave room for future investigation.

## The Ilyran dominion, approximately 430 to 80 years before Saltfall

Ilyr began as a coalition of eastern and southern sea cities. Its people were of several ancestries; “Ilyran” named a political and cultural order, not a vanished species. The early coalition protected shipping, spread a common literary language and joined practical traditions of stone, heat and tide work. Later rulers transformed its fleet stations into garrisons and its alliances into demands for hostages, grain and soldiers.

The dominion was formidable along navigable water and much weaker beyond it. It never ruled all Crownspine, all Redwind or the interior forests. Its admirals called independent coastal communities pirates; those communities called an Ilyran fleet a moving famine. Both accounts could describe the same summer.

Ilyran builders favoured white shell mortar, black stone foundations and long, shallow stairs. Their great works were physical structures organised around rare intersections of the undercurrent. Tide basins softened dangerous surges; heat galleries warmed public baths; stone choirs helped stabilise large foundations. The best works needed teams from several traditions. A fortress commander could order a siege, but could not become a tideworker by holding the right seal.

The wealth behind those marvels included conquest and forced labour. Descendants of displaced communities still recognise their ancestors' quarry marks in admired monuments. Admiration for the engineering does not make the labour voluntary, and condemnation of the dominion does not require every person who lived under it to have been cruel.

## The Divided Reigns, approximately 80 to 1 before Saltfall

Repeated succession wars split the dominion into rival fleets and fortified coasts. Each claimant tried to control the ancient works that sustained harbours and protected lowland fields. Skilled households fled conscription; neglected channels silted; commanders stripped bronze and worked stone from useful structures to repair defences.

The Great Sill already combined a natural rock threshold with channels, gates and immense tide works. It had regulated passage between the western ocean and Inner Salt for centuries. During the final wars, rival commanders attempted to use its different installations against one another. One faction wanted to starve hostile harbours of manageable tides. Another tried to force an opening for a relief fleet. The surviving evidence does not identify a single inventor of the disaster.

At the same time, tremors damaged the western threshold. The evidence supports physical failure worsened by conflicting magical interventions. It does not support a machine capable of destroying any sea at an emperor's whim. The catastrophe required a vulnerable landscape, neglected structures, immense stored pressures and people who continued working after warnings they could not afford to believe.

## Year 0: the Saltfall

The Great Sill broke. Ocean water entered through widened channels in violent surges. Coastal districts subsided; lowland river mouths drowned; islands were separated from the mainland. After the initial catastrophe, the sea settled into a changed basin, leaving some former ports stranded inland and others below navigable water.

The event also tore open the boundary of remembered form at many ancient works. Some sites became inert ruins. Others repeated sound, growth, pressure or heat in dangerous patterns. The wounds did not spread everywhere equally, and they do not all do the same thing. An untouched hillside fifty kilometres from a ruined harbour may show no supernatural disturbance at all.

No single total of the dead is reliable. Whole communities disappeared; survivors carried fragments of names and songs into places that lacked food for them. Some rulers died beside their subjects. Others escaped with soldiers and shut their gates. The Ilyran dominion ended as a governing sea power, though successor houses continued to claim its titles.

## Years 1–58: surviving the sea

The first generations faced salt-poisoned fields, displaced fisheries, epidemics and fighting over higher ground. Forest settlements received refugees while disputing how much canopy could be cleared. Mountain halls opened some galleries and sealed others. Many present villages began as winter camps whose inhabitants found reasons to stay.

Saint Orra was a human healer active approximately 19–58 IS. She travelled among western refugee settlements and taught that an injured stranger should be warmed and tended before anyone demanded an explanation. She died an ordinary death in 58 IS. Later traditions disagree about where she was buried and whether she worked miracles; neither bodily resurrection nor continued personal presence is an established fact. Houses bearing her name often stand on older foundations because the first healers sheltered wherever a roof survived.

The **Winter of Open Doors, 41 IS**, followed harvest failure across several receiving districts. Harbour households, religious houses and some noble estates opened stores and sleeping rooms to outsiders. Other communities refused and survived by doing so. The remembered generosity is real, but celebratory versions often omit the resentment and illness within crowded houses. The winter became a source of both moral example and arguments over who had been abandoned.

## Year 73: the Nine Fires

Successor fleets spent decades fighting over surviving channels and former imperial titles. In 73 IS, a storm drove ships from rival forces onto the same southern approaches. Nine coastal settlements lit warning fires and sent boats for survivors without asking which banner they carried. Their crews then refused demands to return rescued enemies to the water.

The **Nine Fires** did not establish universal peace. They helped end that particular fleet war and gave maritime communities a common expectation that warning lights should serve every approaching vessel. The Nine Beacons later made this inheritance central to their identity. Elsewhere, captains honour it, exploit it or bitterly remember occasions when enemies did neither. A shared custom has force because people choose to uphold it while afraid.

## Years 118–123: the War of the Two Harvests

Northern rulers tried to compel favourable sea access by restricting grain exports after two poor coastal harvests. Merchant fleets answered with raids and seizures; countryside retaliations followed. A conflict initially described as temporary pressure became the **War of the Two Harvests**. The name endured even after fighting outlasted the shortage that began it.

Avarenne could raise armies, but struggled to protect every river mouth. Coastal cities could strike supply routes, but could not feed themselves by victory at sea. Villages were burnt for hiding soldiers they had never invited. Orison's river communities eventually provided neutral meeting places because everybody needed their crossings intact. The settlement of 123 ended the open war through renewed passage and food exchange, not through a permanent settlement of every claim.

The war left a habit of suspicion between granary country and harbour country. It also created border families, mixed veterans' settlements and songs sung on both sides. The people most determined to avoid another war are not always those who speak most kindly of their neighbours.

## Year 191: the Ash Summer

Southern eruptions darkened skies and spoiled harvests in an uneven belt around the Inner Salt. The **Ash Summer** brought cold nights in places accustomed to warm growing seasons, drowned seedlings where rains turned violent, and bitter struggles over winter fodder. Some regions suffered barely at all and became destinations for migration.

The eruptions were physical events. Claims that a particular people's worship caused them are accusations, not established causes. Several coastal rulers nevertheless expelled unwelcome minorities or seized religious property in the panic. Other towns became lasting examples of rescue. Families who moved in 191 still live in districts that call them newcomers nearly sixty years later.

Ash also enriched some southern soils over subsequent decades. Present prosperity can stand on the same ground as remembered starvation. That uncomfortable sequence gives arguments over settlement, gratitude and ownership their emotional force.

## Years 244–250: an unsettled recovery

The **Three Storms of 244 IS** struck in one sailing season. They damaged warning towers, drowned orchards, moved shoals and exposed buried structures. Repairs remain uneven six years later. Wealthy ports rebuilt stone quays while smaller communities patched boats and waited for timber. The storms did not awaken every wound; where erosion uncovered a maintained or contained site, they sometimes made an old danger accessible again.

At the start of 250 IS, no single war consumes the whole region. There are succession anxieties, raids, threatened forests, bitter religious quarrels and towns trying to keep roofs on houses. A young generation sees opportunities its elders call repetitions of disaster. The historical framework ends here. Later stories may advance a local continuity, but their events do not silently become the shared present of every other tale.
